Grammarly wins the day job: cleaner UI, fewer false positives, and suggestions you can accept at speed without breaking flow. ProWritingAid wins on depth — its style, overused-word and sentence-variety reports catch what Grammarly never flags, which fiction and long-form writers will love. Speed-oriented professionals should pick Grammarly; depth-oriented writers should pick ProWritingAid.

Where Grammarly Wins

Grammarly's advantage is trust at speed — you can slam-accept its suggestions with rare regrets.

  • Lowest false-positive rate of any editor we test — critical when you edit hundreds of words a day
  • Everywhere: browser extension caught text in CRM fields, docs and social composers alike
  • Tone detection: flagged unintended sharpness in two emails before send
  • Generative rewrites: safe, on-brand rephrasing that keeps your voice

Where ProWritingAid Wins

ProWritingAid's reports are simply deeper — the analytics a developmental editor would give you.

  • Sentence-variety and readability analytics across a whole manuscript, not paragraph by paragraph
  • Overused-word and cliché reports that fiction testers called 'genuinely humbling'
  • Chapter-scale documents handled natively without the lag Grammarly shows past ~10k words
  • Lifetime license option — cheaper than two years of any subscription

Which Is Better for Professional Work?

On day-to-day professional writing, Grammarly's restraint is the feature. Its suggestions clustered around clarity and correctness, and the acceptance workflow never broke our testers' concentration. Nobody disabled it during the test — the highest praise an editor can get.

ProWritingAid shone on the long documents: the manuscript reports surfaced repetition patterns across chapters and quantified pacing issues that Grammarly's inline model can't see. Fiction testers kept it; corporate testers turned the extra reports off.

Bottom Line

Grammarly is the assistant you forget is there — fast, polite, everywhere — and that invisibility is exactly why professionals keep paying. ProWritingAid is the editor you visit deliberately, the one that hands you a 20-report breakdown of your chapter and a lifetime license. If your writing day is a hundred small documents, buy Grammarly and never think about it again. If your writing year is one manuscript, buy ProWritingAid once and never think about it again either. Both are excellent at their jobs; the only wrong choice is buying the one whose job is not yours.

One last practical note from our test month: whichever you choose, turn off the features you do not use. Half of the complaints we hear about both editors trace to suggestion categories the user never wanted — enabling only grammar, clarity and tone (Grammarly) or your five relevant reports (ProWritingAid) made both tools measurably more pleasant in our retests. Editing software should adapt to your writing, not the reverse.

FAQ

Which is better for non-native English speakers?

ProWritingAid edges it: its suggestions explain the underlying rule, which builds intuition over time, while Grammarly optimizes for silent fixes. That said, Grammarly detects a slightly wider range of ESL error patterns in our testing — fast learners may prefer Grammarly, deliberate improvers ProWritingAid.

Do these tools work offline?

Partially. Grammarly's desktop apps need a connection for suggestions; ProWritingAid's desktop editor analyzes local documents and works offline for its core reports, with cloud features (plagiarism, some AI) requiring a connection.

Is ProWritingAid as good as Grammarly?

Different strengths. Grammarly is better at fast, low-noise everyday correction; ProWritingAid is better at deep style analysis for long-form work. On pure grammar catching they are effectively tied in our tests.

Which is cheaper?

ProWritingAid — $10/mo annual vs Grammarly's $12, plus a lifetime license (~$399) that beats two-plus years of any subscription.

Do they work in Google Docs and Word?

Grammarly covers both plus browsers and mobile keyboards. ProWritingAid covers Word and a desktop/web editor; its browser extension is narrower.

Which is better for fiction writers?

ProWritingAid, clearly. The manuscript-level reports (sentence variety, overused words, pacing) address exactly what fiction editors charge for.

Can they both rewrite with AI?

Yes, both offer AI rewrites on paid tiers. Grammarly's are more conservative and on-voice; ProWritingAid offers more variants per sentence.

How We Tested: Methodology

Our 12-document battery mixed real writing tasks: five business emails, a grant proposal, two university essays, a technical README, a 4,000-word report chapter and two fiction chapters with seeded errors. We hand-counted every suggestion each tool made, classified each as correct, debatable or false positive, and checked what both missed against a professional editor's mark-up.

We also timed acceptance workflows — how long a clean edit pass took per document — and ran both inside Gmail, Google Docs and Word, where integration quality differs sharply between the two.

Common Mistakes When Picking Between Them

The first mistake is buying the deeper tool for everyday email: ProWritingAid's analytics are wasted on a three-line message and its slower flow will quietly annoy you until you switch it off. The second is assuming the free versions compare — Grammarly's free tier is a real editor; ProWritingAid's free tier is a demo.

Third: fiction writers judging by business-document performance. The manuscript reports only earn their keep past a few thousand words of narrative — exactly the use case the comparisons on marketing pages never test.

What the Scores Don't Capture

The 0.4-point gap is really a genre split. On our business documents the two were within 0.2 of each other; on fiction, ProWritingAid pulled far ahead and on everyday-email speed, Grammarly did. A single overall score can't say which side of that split you live on.

Also uncaptured: how each handles non-native English. Grammarly's suggestions assume more; ProWritingAid explains more of its rules. Our multilingual tester preferred the explanations — your team might too.

Who Should Skip Both?

Skip both if you already work with a human editor on everything — no subscription replaces line-editing judgment, and layering tool suggestions under an editor's passes often creates noise. Skip Grammarly if your documents are non-English-heavy: its multilingual support remains shallow, and a localized editor will serve better. Skip ProWritingAid if you never write past a few hundred words — its depth advantages only wake up on long documents, and its slower flow will tax every short email in exchange.
